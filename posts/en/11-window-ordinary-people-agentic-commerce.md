# The Real Window for Ordinary People in Agentic Commerce (No Code, No Capital)

Almost every take on AI and money is aimed at either investors or engineers. But there's a third lane that gets almost no attention: the ordinary person who doesn't code, doesn't have capital, and just wants to build something on the fact that how people buy things is changing. This is that lane, and it's real — not because I say so, but because the mechanics are boring and open.

## What's actually changing

Two things are moving at once, and together they open a door:

1. **Discovery is moving from search to recommendation.** More and more, people don't search for a product and scan results — they ask an AI to recommend one. The AI recommends what it can read and understand. A business that's invisible to an AI is a business that won't be recommended.
2. **Work is moving from humans to agents.** Software increasingly runs multi-step tasks to completion on its own — writing, designing, publishing, coordinating. What used to cost a team, or a big software budget, is becoming something one person can run with tools.

Put those together and you get agentic commerce: a world where machines both find and help fulfill the buy. You don't need to be an engineer or an investor to participate — you need to be *findable* and to sell something that costs you almost nothing to make.

## Why ordinary people aren't locked out

The common fear is that AI just helps big companies and people who can code. The reality is flatter than that. The tools that made this possible are open and cheap: protocols and formats that any site can adopt in an afternoon. The cost of producing things — content, design, digital products — has collapsed. That collapse is the whole point. When the marginal cost of producing is near zero, the person who can produce useful things *and be found* wins, regardless of whether they have a team or a fundraise.

The barrier was never the technology. It was that almost nobody knows the first concrete step to take.

## The concrete steps, in order

1. **Pick one thing to sell that has zero marginal cost.** A guide, a template, a tool, a set of prompts, a workflow — anything you can make once and sell many times. Digital products have no inventory and no shipping.
2. **Put it somewhere people and AI can reach it.** A simple site, a product page, a marketplace listing. The key is that it must be *readable* — a clean, plain-text-friendly structure and an llms.txt file so an AI can understand what you offer.
3. **Write for the questions real people ask.** Don't chase the big head-term keyword. Write about the specific, concrete problems your product solves. Long-tail traffic is lower volume but far higher intent, and it's the kind of traffic that actually converts.
4. **Let the compounding do the work.** You don't need to go viral. A handful of solid, findable pieces that answer real questions will keep being discovered long after you publish them. That's the difference between rented attention and owned attention.

## The honest part

This is not a "get rich in a month" thing, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. The honest picture:

- **It takes time.** SEO-style compounding is real but slow — think months, not days, before discovery builds.
- **It's competitive.** You're not the only person trying this. What saves you is doing it with real substance and real findability, not shortcuts.
- **The bar is findability plus usefulness.** Being easy for an AI to recommend only pays off if what you're recommending is genuinely useful. The two go together.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to learn to code?**
No. Everything described here — a readable site, an llms.txt file, digital products — can be done with free tools and templates. The skills that matter are clarity about what you sell and consistency in producing it.

**How much money do I need to start?**
Effectively none. This lane is specifically about a near-zero-startup-cost business. What you invest is attention and consistency, not capital.

**How long until I see results?**
Honestly, months. The first few pieces won't move much. The advantage compounds as you build a small catalog of findable, useful things. If you're not willing to wait, this lane isn't for you.
